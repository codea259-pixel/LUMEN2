about: Seventh graders read literature and informational texts side by side in six themed collections. They cite several pieces of evidence, analyze how story elements interact, compare characters' points of view, analyze the structure of poems and dramas, compare fictional and historical accounts, trace and evaluate arguments for sound reasoning and sufficient evidence, use phrases, clauses and sentence types for effect, and write narratives, informative essays, arguments, literary analyses and research reports.

std: RL.7.2 | Determine a theme or central idea of a text and analyze its development over the course of the text; provide an objective summary of the text.
std: RL.7.3 | Analyze how particular elements of a story or drama interact (e.g., how setting shapes the characters or plot).
std: RI.7.2 | Determine two or more central ideas in a text and analyze their development over the course of the text; provide an objective summary of the text.
std: RI.7.8 | Trace and evaluate the argument and specific claims in a text, assessing whether the reasoning is sound and the evidence is relevant and sufficient to support the claims.
std: L.7.1 | Demonstrate command of the conventions of standard English grammar and usage, including the function of phrases and clauses and simple, compound, complex and compound-complex sentences.
std: W.7.1 | Write arguments to support claims with clear reasons and relevant evidence.
std: W.7.2 | Write informative/explanatory texts to examine a topic and convey ideas, concepts and information through the selection, organization and analysis of relevant content.

passage: Saturday Nights | Every Saturday night from April to October, the parking lot behind the library turned into a night market. White tents went up in rows, strings of bulbs glowed overhead, and the air filled with smoke from the grills and the sound of six languages at once. For as long as Tuan could remember, his family's noodle stand had stood in the third row, between a woman who sold phone cases and a man who sold grilled corn. // Tuan was thirteen, which meant he was old enough to take orders, count change and carry steaming bowls without spilling. It also meant, according to his father, that he was old enough to understand that the stand paid for everything: rent, his sister's braces, even the trumpet Tuan practiced every afternoon. // The problem was the jazz band. The community youth band rehearsed on Saturday nights, and the director, Ms. Abernathy, had heard him play at a school concert. "We need a second trumpet," she had said. "Think about it." // Tuan thought about it every Saturday as he ladled broth. He did not ask his father. He already knew the answer. Saturday was the busiest night. Without Tuan, his mother would have to work the register and the stove at once. // Then, in late June, the man who sold grilled corn did not come back. In his place, a group of teenagers set up a small stage with a sign: OPEN MIC, ALL WELCOME. // For three weeks, Tuan listened to singers and a boy with a violin. On the fourth Saturday, during a slow hour, his mother nudged him. "Your horn is in the car," she said quietly. "Go." // "Dad will..." // "Your father is in the back. Go." // Tuan played one song, an old ballad his grandfather used to hum. He was halfway through when he saw his father standing at the edge of the crowd, still in his apron, arms folded. Tuan's heart stopped, but his fingers kept moving. // When he finished, people clapped. His father walked back to the stand without a word. // That night, as they packed up the tents, his father finally spoke. "That band," he said, wiping down the counter. "What time does it end?" // "Nine," Tuan said. // His father nodded slowly. "After nine, I still need you to help pack up." He paused. "Your mother says your cousin Linh wants to earn money. Maybe she can take your Saturday shift." // Tuan could not speak, so he picked up a stack of chairs instead. His father was not a man of many words. But Tuan understood that this was a turning point, for both of them.

Use at least two details. | The busy Saturday market creates the conflict because the stand needs Tuan when the band rehearses. passage: A Moldy Turning Point | In September 1928, a Scottish scientist named Alexander Fleming returned to his London laboratory after a summer vacation. Before he left, he had stacked dishes of bacteria on a bench. Now, sorting through them, he noticed something odd. In one dish, a fuzzy patch of mold had grown. Around the mold was a clear ring where the bacteria had died. // Many scientists might have thrown the dish away. Fleming did not. He was curious about why the bacteria near the mold had disappeared. He grew more of the mold, a type called Penicillium, and found that it released a substance that killed many kinds of harmful bacteria. He named the substance penicillin. // Fleming's discovery was a turning point, but it did not change medicine right away. Penicillin was difficult to produce and purify, and Fleming could not make enough of it to treat patients. For about ten years, his discovery attracted little attention. // The next turning point came at the University of Oxford. Around 1939, a team led by Howard Florey and Ernst Chain began studying penicillin again. Working with other scientists, including Norman Heatley, who designed clever equipment to extract the drug, they found a way to purify it. In 1940, they showed that penicillin protected mice from deadly infections. Soon they tested it on human patients, with remarkable results. // World War II added urgency. Wounded soldiers often died from infected injuries, and penicillin could save them. British and American scientists and companies worked together to mass-produce the drug. By the end of the war, penicillin was being made in large quantities. // In 1945, Fleming, Florey and Chain shared the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine. Penicillin became the first widely used antibiotic and has since saved millions of lives. // The story of penicillin is often told as a lucky accident. Luck mattered. But the discovery became a turning point only because of a chain of people: one curious scientist who noticed something strange, a team that refused to give up on a difficult problem, and a world that urgently needed what they made.

passage: Number 42 | On April 15, 1947, a twenty-eight-year-old first baseman trotted onto Ebbets Field in Brooklyn, New York. His name was Jackie Robinson, and by playing that day for the Brooklyn Dodgers, he became the first Black player in modern Major League Baseball. For decades, an unwritten rule had kept Black athletes out of the major leagues, forcing them to play in separate leagues. Robinson's first game marked a turning point not only for baseball but for the nation. // The turning point was carefully planned. Branch Rickey, the Dodgers' president, had wanted to integrate the team for years. He searched for a player who was not only talented but also able to stay calm under enormous pressure. Robinson, who had been a star athlete at UCLA and had served in the Army, seemed like the right choice. According to accounts of their first meeting, Rickey warned Robinson that he would face insults and threats and asked him to promise not to fight back, at least at first. Rickey wanted a player with the courage not to respond. // Robinson kept that promise, but it came at a cost. Some opposing players shouted cruel insults. Pitchers threw at him. He received hate mail and threats. Even a few of his own teammates at first signed a petition saying they did not want to play with him. Through it all, Robinson answered with his performance. He hit, ran and stole bases with a daring style that thrilled fans. At the end of the 1947 season, he was named the first winner of the league's Rookie of the Year award. // His success changed minds. Teammates who had doubted him came to defend him. Other teams began signing Black players. Within a few years, several of the game's biggest stars were players who would have been shut out entirely just a decade earlier. // Robinson's influence extended far beyond the ballpark. After retiring, he spoke out for civil rights and worked in business to create opportunities for Black Americans. In 1997, fifty years after his first game, Major League Baseball retired his uniform number, 42, for every team. Today, on April 15 each year, every major league player wears number 42 to honor him. // Robinson's gravestone carries his belief that a life is not important except in the impact it has on other lives. By that measure, his was one of the most important lives in American sports.

passage: Second Chair | For two years, Priya had sat in the first chair of the Washington Middle School orchestra's violin section. First chair meant playing the solos. It meant tuning the orchestra before every concert, standing alone while everyone waited for her single, perfect A. It meant, Priya thought, that she was the best. // Then, in October, a new girl named Ximena arrived from Guadalajara. She was still learning English, and on her first day she sat in the very last chair, holding her violin case on her lap as if it might be taken away. At the November seating audition, she played a piece by Bach so beautifully that Mr. Okonkwo, the orchestra director, set down his pencil and simply listened. // The next day, the new seating chart was posted. Ximena: first chair. Priya: second. // Priya told herself she did not care. She told her mother she did not care. But at rehearsals she played a little too loudly, and when Ximena glanced over for help with a word in the music, like "ritardando" or "staccato," Priya pretended not to notice. // The winter concert came. The auditorium was full, and the stage lights were hot and bright. Mr. Okonkwo nodded to Ximena to stand and tune the orchestra. Ximena stood. She raised her bow. And nothing happened. // Priya saw Ximena's hand shaking. She saw her eyes move across the enormous crowd, her face pale. Someone in the audience coughed. The silence stretched: one second, two, three. // Priya knew that feeling. She remembered her own first concert, when her knees had shaken so badly that she had been sure the whole audience could see. She remembered the eighth grader who had leaned over and whispered, "Just look at your strings." // Before she could think about it, Priya leaned toward Ximena. "Mira las cuerdas," she whispered, using the little Spanish she had learned from her best friend. "Look at your strings." // Ximena looked down. She took a breath. Then she drew her bow across the string, and a clear, steady A filled the hall. // After the concert, Ximena found Priya backstage. "Gracias," she said, and then, carefully, in English: "Thank you. You helped me." // "You helped me too," Priya said, and she was surprised to find that she meant it. She was not sure exactly what Ximena had helped her with. But the second chair, she realized, felt a lot lighter than it had that morning.
