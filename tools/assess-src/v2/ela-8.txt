about: Eighth graders read literature and informational texts side by side in six themed collections. They cite the strongest evidence, analyze how dialogue and incidents propel action and reveal character, explain how differences in point of view create suspense or humor, analyze how modern works draw on myths and traditional stories, delineate and evaluate arguments, analyze texts that present conflicting information, use verbals, active and passive voice and verb moods for effect, and write arguments, informative essays, narratives, literary analyses and research reports.

passage: Waiting for the Second Marshmallow | In the late 1960s, psychologist Walter Mischel and his colleagues at Stanford University began a simple experiment. A young child sat at a table with a treat, often a marshmallow. The researcher made an offer: the child could eat the treat right away, or wait alone for about fifteen minutes and receive two treats instead. Then the researcher left the room. // Some children ate the marshmallow almost immediately. Others covered their eyes, sang to themselves or turned their chairs around so they could not see it. Some managed to wait the whole time. // Years later, Mischel's team followed up with some of the children. They reported that the children who had waited longer tended, as teenagers, to have higher scores on college entrance exams and to be described by their parents as better at handling stress. The "marshmallow test" became famous. Books and articles suggested that the ability to delay gratification, to give up a reward now for a larger one later, might be a key to success in life. // But science keeps asking questions. In 2012, researchers at the University of Rochester added a twist. Before the marshmallow test, children worked on an art project with an adult. For some children, the adult kept promises, bringing better art supplies as promised. For others, the adult broke the promises. Children who had experienced a reliable adult waited far longer for the second marshmallow than those who had experienced an unreliable one. Waiting, it seemed, depended partly on whether a child believed the reward would really come. // In 2018, another team repeated the original study with a much larger and more diverse group of children. They found a link between waiting and later achievement, but it was much smaller than earlier reports suggested. Once the researchers accounted for factors such as family income and home environment, the link shrank further. // Together, these studies paint a more complicated picture than the original headlines. Self-control matters, but it does not exist in a vacuum. A child who grows up where promises are often broken may reasonably decide that one marshmallow in hand is worth more than two in the future. Rather than simply telling children to "try harder," the newer research suggests that adults can help by creating environments where waiting actually pays off. // The marshmallow test is still a valuable tool. Its biggest lesson, though, may be about how science works: an exciting finding is the beginning of the conversation, not the end.

passage: The Power of the Default | Every day you make hundreds of choices: what to eat, what to wear, which button to click. You probably think you make most of those choices on your own. But researchers who study decision-making have found that the way a choice is presented can have a surprisingly powerful effect on what people choose. // Consider the "default," the option that takes effect if a person does nothing. In 2003, researchers Eric Johnson and Daniel Goldstein compared organ donation rates in European countries. In some countries, people were organ donors only if they signed up, or "opted in." In others, everyone was considered a donor unless they "opted out." The difference was dramatic. In Germany, an opt-in country, only about 12 percent of people had agreed to be donors. In neighboring Austria, an opt-out country, the figure was over 99 percent. The two countries have much in common, yet a simple difference in the default produced enormously different results. // Defaults affect money decisions too. When some U.S. companies began automatically enrolling new employees in retirement savings plans, while allowing them to leave the plan if they chose, participation rose sharply compared with plans that required workers to sign up. // Why do defaults matter so much? Researchers suggest several reasons. Making a choice takes effort, and people often put off decisions. People may also see the default as a recommendation, assuming it was chosen for a good reason. And switching away from the default can feel like a risk. // In 2008, economist Richard Thaler and legal scholar Cass Sunstein published a book called Nudge, which described how small changes in the way choices are presented can steer people toward better decisions without taking away their freedom to choose. Thaler later won the Nobel Prize in economics in 2017, partly for this work. // Not everyone is comfortable with nudges. Critics point out that whoever designs a choice decides which way people are nudged, and that power can be misused, for example by companies that make it easy to sign up for a subscription but hard to cancel. Supporters respond that every choice must be presented in some way, so designers might as well choose defaults that help people. // Understanding defaults can make you a sharper decision-maker. The next time you face a pre-checked box or an automatic setting, ask yourself: is this what I would choose if I had to decide on my own?

She said it as if it were a fair trade. // Leo wasn't sure it was. // On the last day of school, he put the FocusBand back in its box. His mom asked why. // "I think my brain needs to get distracted sometimes," he said. "That's where the dragons come from." // She looked at him for a moment, then at the tests on the refrigerator. She didn't argue. // That summer, Leo filled two notebooks. His drawings were messy and strange, and some of them were not very good. He didn't care. Every one of them felt like his.

passage: A Book That Changed the World: Silent Spring | In the 1940s and 1950s, a chemical called DDT seemed like a miracle. During World War II it had helped protect soldiers from insects that spread diseases such as malaria and typhus. After the war, farmers, cities and homeowners sprayed it widely to kill crop pests and mosquitoes. Trucks fogged neighborhoods with it while children played nearby. // Rachel Carson, a marine biologist who had worked for the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, grew alarmed. She gathered research from scientists across the country and found troubling evidence that DDT and similar pesticides were harming wildlife. The chemicals did not simply disappear after spraying. They built up in the bodies of animals, becoming more concentrated as they moved up the food chain, from insects to fish to birds. // In 1962, Carson published her findings in a book called Silent Spring. The title came from a haunting image: a spring morning with no birdsong, because the birds had been poisoned. Carson wrote clearly for ordinary readers, and the book became a bestseller. // Some chemical companies attacked the book and Carson personally, calling her claims exaggerated. But President John F. Kennedy asked his science advisory committee to examine the issue, and its 1963 report largely supported her concerns about pesticides. // Carson died of cancer in 1964, before she could see the full impact of her work. Public concern about pollution continued to grow. In 1970, the U.S. government created the Environmental Protection Agency. In 1972, the agency banned most uses of DDT in the United States. // Later research confirmed one of the most serious effects. DDT caused some birds, including bald eagles and peregrine falcons, to lay eggs with shells so thin that they cracked before hatching. As DDT use ended and protections increased, these birds slowly recovered. In 2007, the bald eagle was removed from the federal list of endangered and threatened species. // The story is not entirely simple. In some parts of the world, health experts still debate the limited use of DDT inside homes to fight malaria, a disease that kills hundreds of thousands of people each year. Balancing human health and environmental protection remains difficult. // But Silent Spring changed how many people think about science and responsibility. It showed that new technologies can have consequences no one intended, and that careful research, clearly communicated, can help the public make wiser choices.
