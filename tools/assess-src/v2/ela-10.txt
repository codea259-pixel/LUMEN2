passage: How a Bill Becomes a Law | Every federal law in the United States, from rules about clean water to the date of a national holiday, began as an idea that had to pass through a long and demanding process. The Constitution gives Congress, which is made up of the House of Representatives and the Senate, the power to make federal laws. Understanding the path a bill takes helps explain why many proposals never become law. // The process begins when a member of Congress introduces a bill. A bill can start in either the House or the Senate, though the Constitution requires bills for raising revenue to begin in the House. The bill is then sent to a committee, a smaller group of members who specialize in a topic such as agriculture or education. Committees may hold hearings, invite experts to testify and revise the bill. Most bills never make it out of committee. // If the committee approves the bill, it goes to the full chamber, where members debate it and vote. A bill that passes one chamber then moves to the other, where it goes through the same steps. Both chambers must pass exactly the same version. If their versions differ, members work out a compromise, sometimes in a conference committee, and both chambers vote again. // Finally, the bill goes to the President. If the President signs it, it becomes law. If the President vetoes, or rejects, the bill, Congress can still make it law by overriding the veto with a two-thirds vote in both the House and the Senate. If the President takes no action for ten days, not counting Sundays, while Congress is in session, the bill becomes law without a signature. But if Congress adjourns during those ten days and the President has not signed it, the bill dies; this is called a pocket veto. // This slow, careful process is not an accident. The framers of the Constitution designed it so that laws would need broad agreement and so that each branch could check the power of the others.
