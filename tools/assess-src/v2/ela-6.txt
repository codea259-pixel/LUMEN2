passage: The Last Rung | Every summer since I was eight, I had climbed exactly three rungs of the high-dive ladder at the Mariposa Street pool and then climbed back down. Three rungs was my record. I told everyone I was "warming up." Nobody believed me, but my friends were kind enough to pretend. // This summer was supposed to be different. I was twelve now, taller than my mom, and I had promised myself on the first day of June that I would jump before school started. It was now the last Saturday in August. // My little sister, Rosa, sat on the edge of the pool with her feet in the water. She was six and afraid of almost nothing, which I found deeply unfair. "Are you going to do it today, Teo?" she asked, loud enough for the lifeguard to hear. // "Maybe," I said. My stomach answered for me by twisting into a knot. // I climbed. One rung, two, three. My hands were slippery, and the metal was hot from the sun. On the fourth rung, I made the mistake of looking down. The pool looked like a blue postage stamp. I froze, and for a long moment the only sound I heard was my own breathing. // Then I heard Rosa. She was not shouting or teasing. She was counting, slowly and calmly, the way our grandmother counts when she braids our hair. "Four... five... six..." // I realized she was counting the rungs I had left. I did not decide to keep climbing so much as my feet decided for me, one number at a time. When Rosa said "ten," I was standing on the board. // The board bounced a little under my feet. I walked to the end, closed my eyes and stepped off before my brain could argue. The fall lasted about one second and also about a year. Then the water closed over me, cool and loud, and I came up laughing. // Rosa was clapping. The lifeguard gave me a thumbs-up. I swam to the edge, breathing hard, and Rosa leaned over. // "Can I count for you again?" she asked. // I climbed the ladder four more times that afternoon. I never looked down once, but I listened for her voice every time.

passage: Your Brain on Fear | Imagine you are walking on a trail and see a long, curved shape in the path. Before you have time to think "snake," your heart is pounding and you have jumped backward. A moment later, you look again and see that it is only a stick. You may feel silly, but your brain did exactly what it was built to do. // Fear is one of the body's oldest alarm systems. Deep inside the brain is a small, almond-shaped area called the amygdala. Scientists have found that the amygdala plays a key role in noticing possible danger. When it senses a threat, it helps send signals that prepare the body to act. // Those signals set off what is often called the "fight-or-flight" response. The adrenal glands release a hormone called adrenaline. Your heart beats faster to send more blood to your muscles. Your breathing speeds up to bring in more oxygen. Your senses may feel sharper. All of these changes happen in seconds, and they prepare you either to face a danger or to get away from it. // This system is fast because speed can save lives. A person who jumps back from a stick loses nothing. A person who waits too long to react to a real snake could be hurt. In other words, the brain would rather make a harmless mistake than a dangerous one. // However, the same alarm can go off when there is no real danger at all. Many people feel their hearts race before giving a speech, taking a test or trying out for a team. These situations are not life-threatening, but the brain may react as if they were. // The good news is that people can teach their alarm systems to calm down. Psychologists have found that facing a feared situation in small, manageable steps often makes the fear weaker over time. Someone afraid of speaking in front of a class might first practice alone, then in front of one friend, then in front of a small group. Each success shows the brain that the situation is safe. // Fear, then, is not a weakness. It is a tool. Understanding how it works can help people use it wisely, listening to it when danger is real and gently retraining it when it is not.
mc2: Which sentence best states the central idea of "Your Brain on Fear"? | Fear is a fast alarm system that protects us, and people can learn to calm it when there is no real danger. | The amygdala is a small, almond-shaped part of the brain. | Snakes are the most common cause of fear on hiking trails. | People should avoid all situations that make them nervous.

mc2: How does the author illustrate the idea of facing fear "in small, manageable steps"? | with the example of practicing a speech alone, then with a friend, then with a group | with a list of studies that psychologists have done on fear | with a second story about a snake on a trail | with a definition of the amygdala and its job
mc3: Which statement would the author most likely agree with? | A racing heart before a test is a normal reaction, not a sign of weakness. | People who feel fear are less brave than other people. | The amygdala should be trained to stop working. | Fear is useful only for wild animals.
sa2: Write an objective summary of "Your Brain on Fear" in two or three sentences. | The article explains that fear is an alarm system, involving the amygdala, that triggers the fight-or-flight response to prepare the body for danger. passage: Partners in the Rubble | When a building collapses after an earthquake, rescuers face a race against time. People may be trapped under concrete and twisted metal, and no one knows exactly where. In these moments, one of the most valuable members of a rescue team has four legs and a powerful nose. // Search-and-rescue dogs are trained to find people by scent. Every person constantly sheds tiny skin cells and gives off odors that a dog can detect. A dog's nose contains far more scent receptors than a human nose, so it can notice smells that people cannot. By following the strongest scent, a trained dog can lead rescuers to the spot where someone is buried. // Training a rescue dog takes patience. Most teams begin with a game. A trainer hides just out of sight, and the puppy is rewarded with a toy or praise when it finds the trainer. Little by little, the hiding places become harder: behind boxes, under piles of wood, inside dark spaces. Eventually the dog learns to search large areas and to bark when it finds a person. Many dogs train for a year or more before they are ready for a real disaster. // The dogs are not the only ones who must be brave. Their handlers travel into unstable buildings, flooded streets and smoky ruins. They learn first aid, map reading and safety rules. Most important, they learn to read their dogs. A slight change in a dog's tail or ears might be the first sign that it has caught a scent. // The work requires trust in both directions. The dog must trust that its handler will keep it safe on shifting ground. The handler must trust the dog's nose, even when nothing seems to be there. Handlers often describe moments when a dog stopped at a pile of rubble that looked empty, and a person was found underneath. They learn to believe their partners. // Not every search ends with a rescue. But when a dog's bark leads to a survivor, the team's long months of practice suddenly make sense. Courage, these teams show, is not just a feeling in a dangerous moment. It is built slowly, one practice search at a time.

Every year, when the dry season came, the old bridge was cut down and a new one was woven, for grass rope grows weak with time. // Amaru was the finest young weaver in the village. His fingers were quick, his knots were tight, and he knew it. "When I am grown," he often said, "I will weave the bridge myself, and people will sing about me." // That year, a storm tore the old bridge from its posts before the dry season arrived. The village was cut off. The elders called a meeting to plan the weaving, but Amaru did not go. He had decided that this was his chance. That night, alone in his family's storehouse, he began to braid a great rope from the grass he had gathered. // He worked for three days and three nights. His hands blistered. His eyes burned. On the fourth morning, he dragged his rope to the edge of the canyon and threw one end across to show everyone what he had done. // It fell short. It dangled over the river, swinging, a full stone's throw from the other side. And where he had braided too fast, the strands had already begun to fray. // Amaru sat down on the rocks, ashamed. After a while, his grandmother sat beside him. She picked up the frayed end of his rope and turned it in her fingers. // "These are good knots," she said. "But a bridge is not one rope. It is many cords twisted into one. That is why it holds." // The next day, every family in the village came to the canyon, each carrying a cord braided at home: thin ones from children, thick ones from the strongest men and women, uneven ones from the very old. Amaru's task was to twist them together. He worked slowly now, listening to the elders and testing each twist. // When the new bridge stretched across the canyon, the people did sing, as Amaru had once dreamed. But they did not sing his name. They sang the names of every family whose cord was in the bridge. // Amaru found that he did not mind. When he crossed the swaying bridge for the first time, he could feel each cord beneath his feet, holding.
